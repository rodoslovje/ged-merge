GED Merge User's Guide

A GEDCOM file (.ged) is the standard format genealogy programs use to store a family tree — the people, families, events, sources and photos that make up your research. GED Merge is a complete GEDCOM editor that runs entirely in your browser, so nothing is uploaded. Use it to edit your family tree, compare and merge a second file into it, and maintain your main file with a suite of clean-up tools.

Part I — Getting Started

1What GED Merge is

GED Merge opens one or two GEDCOM files (the standard family-tree exchange format, .ged) and lets you work on them through three modes, switched from the tabs at the top:

  • Edit — browse and directly change your own tree: names, events, families, notes, sources and photos. No second file required.
  • Merge — load a second tree (or a matches CSV) and fold confirmed records into your own, field by field.
  • Tools — maintaining your main file as a whole: a health check, duplicate finder, style normalizer, a privacy redactor, and source/place browsers.

Everything happens locally. Reading, matching, merging and editing all run inside your browser on your own computer — your file is never uploaded anywhere. (Map backgrounds and place lookup fetch from public map services; only a place name is ever sent, never your tree.) Your loaded file is never modified in place — you always download a new copy when you save (see §14).

Tip

New here? On the landing page, open one of the sample trees (Europe Royal Families, Tudors, US Presidents) to explore every feature with real data before loading your own file.

2The workspace

The app shell is the same in every mode. From the top bar you can:

  • Mode tabs — switch between Edit, Merge and Tools (shortcuts E M T).
  • File pills — the loaded main and incoming file names. Click a pill to see or replace that file. On a phone the names are in the ☰ menu instead.
  • Undo / Redo — one shared history across edits and merge decisions (CtrlZ / CtrlShiftZ).
  • Save GEDCOM — preview and download your changes (CtrlS).
  • Search everyone — the ⌕ button (or /) finds any person in the file by name; Enter opens them, ShiftEnter opens their charts. Filters narrow a common name down: sex, a birth-year range, a place, whether the person has links, notes or sources, whether you have edited them, their merge decision, and how close they are to your start person (§7). If the search finds nobody, it offers to add the name you typed as a new person.
  • Add new person — the ＋ button (or N) creates a person linked to nobody, from any mode — see §8.
  • Settings — the ⚙ menu holds appearance (theme and language), name and record display, and data options — see Settings below.
Note

The app is built for tablet and desktop screens. On a phone you can still review, but editing large trees is more comfortable on a wider display. There the top bar is a single row — the mode tabs and a ☰ menu holding the file names, the start person, and the Charts, Search, Add person and Settings buttons — with Save and ↩ ↪ on a second row whenever there is something to save or undo. The pickers that do not fit across the screen — the Tools sub-tools and the chart-kind switcher — become dropdowns naming the one you are on. The page scrolls as one: the header stays at the top and the footer sits at the end of the content, and a full-page chart or map takes the whole screen, without either of them.

GED Merge is keyboard-driven — press ? anywhere to open the full shortcut sheet, or see §16.

Settings

The ⚙ Settings menu has four tabs. General covers everyday preferences; GEDCOM lets you pin the file conventions GED Merge otherwise detects automatically; Map controls the map background and the additional map layers; and Advanced holds the online lookups and the locally stored data. Every choice is remembered in this browser.

General — four groups, in this order:

  • Language — English or Slovenščina.
  • Appearance — Theme: Automatic, Light or Dark (Automatic follows your operating system).
  • Name display — Name order (given name first or surname first), Uppercase surnames (e.g. Ana NOVAK) and Show married surname, which appends it in parentheses, e.g. Ana Novak (Kovač). A live sample above the options shows the result. These change how names display everywhere; your stored data is untouched.
  • Display — Show kinship shows each person's relationship to your start person (§7) throughout the app; Show record IDs puts each person's GEDCOM record id (xref) next to their name; and Show ages adds ages after lifespans and beside event dates.

The other three tabs:

  • GEDCOM — override any file convention GED Merge would otherwise detect, grouped as Dates, Names, Places, Sources and Privacy: date format, unknown date parts, alternate names, the unknown-name marker, place format, source and citation layout, page links, baptism citations, doubled links, the Matricula and Geneanet link languages, and the private-flag tag. Each row shows a sample of what the choice writes and defaults to Auto (detected) — following the file's own habit — but a chosen value is then enforced by the incoming-file normalizer, Organize sources, Add Source and inline editing. Date, place, name and link choices take effect from the next file load. See §4 and §13.
  • Map — Load base-map tiles opts in to a map background for every map in the app (tile requests reveal the viewed region; off = a built-in offline outline), and Base map picks which one: CARTO, OpenStreetMap, terrain, Esri satellite or topographic, or your own tile-URL template. Once tiles are allowed, a live sample above the picker draws the current choice. Additional map layers configures maps drawn over the base — pick a bundled free preset (including Slovenia's national GURS maps), or add your own tile-URL template or OGC WMS service (name, URL, valid years, attribution). See §11.
  • Advanced › Online lookups — Look up details online: when you add a source from a bare URL, GED Merge fetches its page title through a public relay. The same switch powers the one-click OpenStreetMap and GURS gazetteer downloads, the per-row online searches (OpenStreetMap and the Genealogical Gazetteer, GOV) and the GURS address-register lookups used by Geocode places; those services are called directly, not through a relay. Apart from the map tiles you opt into separately, these are the only features that send anything off your device, and they are off by default.
  • Advanced › Locally stored data — Remember my work in this browser and Clear locally stored data.

Keeping your work & working offline

By default GED Merge keeps nothing between visits — reloading starts fresh. Turn on Settings › Advanced › Remember my work in this browser to cache your loaded files and your review and edit progress locally, so a reload restores exactly where you left off. The browser may ask permission to keep the data; nothing ever leaves your device. Clear locally stored data in the same panel removes everything this browser holds about your file — the cached files and progress, the place lookups you accepted, and the link to your media folder. Imported gazetteers and your settings stay, and the file you currently have open stays open.

GED Merge is an installable progressive web app. Use your browser's Install or Add to Home Screen action to run it in its own window; once loaded it works offline. When a new version is published, a small banner offers to Reload.

3Loading files

Drag a file onto the drop zone, or click to browse. GED Merge works with two files, and which is which matters throughout the guide:

  • Main file — your primary family tree, the one you own and maintain. Load it first; Edit and Tools always work on it.
  • Incoming — what you compare the main file against, loaded only in Merge mode: a second .ged, or a matches CSV exported from indeks.rodoslovje.si (the Slovenian genealogical index).

Neither original is ever changed on disk: every edit and every merge builds a new copy that you download (§14).

No GEDCOM to load? Start a new tree, under the sample trees, begins an empty file, and Edit then offers to add its first person — see §8. It behaves like any other loaded file from there on: you build it up person by person and download it with Save GEDCOM.

After a file loads, the file-info panel reports what was read — individual and family counts, GEDCOM version, encoding, and the file's detected house style: its date format, place layout, source layout and name layout (explained in §4). For an incoming file it also lists how many dates, places, links and names were reshaped to match your main file, and how many split copies of the same person were merged into one.

Tip

If your tree references local photos, GED Merge offers to connect a media folder so it can display them. See §12.

Part II — Core Concepts

4House style & normalization

Every GEDCOM has its own conventions — its house style. GED Merge detects four of them:

  • Date format — e.g. 01.01.1900 vs 1 JAN 1900, including how unknown parts are marked.
  • Place layout — whether places are one packed line, a structured comma hierarchy, a separate street address, and so on.
  • Source layout — bibliographic citations, repository links, page-image sources (Matricula-style), or inline text.
  • Name layout — whether alternate names live as inline sub-tags or as separate typed name records.

When you load an incoming file, GED Merge normalizes it to your main file's house style before comparing — reformatting its dates, reshaping its places, rewriting links (Matricula, Geneanet) and reshaping name variants — so the comparison is like-for-like and anything you merge in already matches your file's conventions. The Normalize tool applies the same idea within a single (main) file, bringing outliers in line with the file's own dominant style.

GED Merge also reconciles the two files' GEDCOM version. If the incoming file uses a different version than your main file (5.5.1 vs 7.0), it is migrated to your file's version on load: shared notes (NOTE ⇄ SNOTE), date phrases and dual-year dates, age keywords, association roles, legacy identifiers (AFN/RFN ⇄ EXID), transliterated names and media types are all rewritten to the target spec, so merged records stay valid. The load summary lists what was migrated, with examples on hover.

Tip

Every one of these conventions can be pinned by hand in Settings › GEDCOM (§2) when you'd rather choose the target style than let GED Merge detect it.

5Sources, media & lossless data

GED Merge reads the whole file losslessly: every line is preserved, including custom and non-standard tags, so nothing is silently dropped when you save. Beyond people and families, that includes sources (repositories, source records and citations), media objects (photos and document images), and the notes and links attached to any record. These are first-class throughout: you can add and edit them in Edit (§13, §12) and browse them across your main file in Tools.

6The match score

When both files are loaded, GED Merge automatically pairs individuals between them and gives each pair a match score from 0 to 100%. The score weighs several signals: surname, given name, birth and death date and place, parents, partners, children and marriage. Implausible pairs are filtered out early (for example, an irreconcilable surname or an impossible age gap), so the list surfaces genuine candidates.

A high score means the two records are very likely the same person; a middling score is worth a careful look. The Find duplicates tool uses the very same engine to spot two records inside one (main) file that describe the same person.

7Start person & kinship

Set a start person — usually yourself or the root of your tree — and GED Merge ranks the matches it finds (§6) by kinship distance: how many relationship hops separate each candidate from your start person. Your closest relatives then surface first, and each match shows its relationship (e.g. Grandfather, 2nd cousin). A sensible start person is chosen automatically when a file loads; you can change or clear it at any time. Go to start person jumps the current view back to them at any moment.

Kinship labels are colour-coded by blood line: relatives on the father's side show in blue, the mother's side in violet, while full siblings (“both lines”), descendants and the start person keep the neutral green accent. Kinship also recognizes half-siblings and step-relations (step-parent, step-child, step-sibling) with their own labels. The colours appear wherever kinship shows — person headers, relative cards, tree nodes and the relationship diagram (see the legend, §15).

Part III — The Three Modes

8Edit — your main GEDCOM file

Navigating

Switch to Edit (E) to browse and modify your main file directly — no second file needed. Pick anyone from the Jump to person box, or click a relative's card to walk the tree; Back returns to where you came from. If the current person also matches your incoming file, Merge in the toolbar jumps straight to that comparison, and the person header carries the same three decision buttons — Confirm, Reject and Defer — so a match can be decided, or a decision changed, without leaving Edit. What the three mean is in §9. Every change is held in memory and reversible with CtrlZ until you download.

The toolbar's Charts button (V) opens the eight diagrams, maps and reports for whoever is on screen — they are a shared feature rather than part of editing, and are covered in §11.

Names & identity

Given name, surname, sex and nickname edit inline — click a field, type, click away to save. The × glyph clears a field. + Add Name attaches alternative name variants (married name, name at birth, aka, formal and more), each with its own given name, surname and type; the × glyph removes a variant.

Events

Birth is always shown. + Add Event reveals the rest, grouped by life stage:
  • Early Life — Baptism, Christening, First Communion, Confirmation, Adoption. Baptism is baptism in general (GEDCOM BAPM), Christening the baptism and naming of a child (CHR); GED Merge treats Baptism as the usual one, and it is what the baptism-citation setting writes to.
  • Career — Occupation, Education, Graduation, Retirement, Military service
  • Attributes — Title, Physical description, Religion, Nationality, Race / ethnicity, Number of children, Award, Legal transaction, Deed, Medical, Illness, Reference number. These are GEDCOM attributes rather than events: most carry a value on the row itself (1 OCCU Farmer) as well as the usual date, place and sources.
  • Residence & Movement — Residence, Emigration, Immigration, Naturalization, Census
  • Estate — Will, Probate, and the two generic kinds, Event and Fact
  • Death — Death, Cremation, Burial, Interment, Funeral

An event row shows only the fields it actually carries, starting with the date and the place. + Add at the end of the row adds the rest one at a time — address, agency, source, type, cause, note — and puts the cursor straight in the new field, so the row never becomes a wall of empty inputs. Use × to clear a single field.

To change an event's type, copy it or remove the whole event, use the dropdown menu on its event-type label. Copy event to… opens a checkbox list of the records the event can go to — people for a personal event, families for a family event, with the person's own household listed first — and hands the whole event over at once: date, place with its coordinate, address, notes and sources. It is how a newly recorded residence reaches the rest of the family in one step; a record that already carries the event is listed but cannot be picked.

A custom event (a GEDCOM EVEN/FACT such as “Civil Partnership”) reads under its own Title — edited in an always-visible field on the event row — instead of a generic label, and is available from the + Add Event menu on both a person and a family. Beyond this menu, GED Merge reads and preserves many other programs' vendor-specific events and facts, and surfaces those as editable, mergeable rows too (see §14).

A place your file has never used

The place field suggests places already in your file as you type. When none of them fits — a village entered for the first time — the list ends with Look this place up in the registers, and nothing is asked until you click it. It always searches the gazetteer you imported in Geocode places. The three online registers — OpenStreetMap, Slovenia's address register (GURS) and the Genealogical Gazetteer (GOV) — are only asked when Look up details online is on in Settings › Advanced; with it off the row says so and answers from the imported gazetteer alone.

Each offer is already written the way your file writes places: the same layout, the same comma separator, the same spelling of the country, and the same number of jurisdiction levels — a file that writes Kranj,Kranj,Slovenija is offered Zabukovje,Sevnica,Slovenija, a file that writes only locality and country is offered Zabukovje,Slovenija. A badge names the answering register. Picking one fills in the place, its address when you typed a house number the address register recognised, and the coordinate that register puts it at — in one step you can undo as one.

The address field has the same row, and looks the house up inside the event's place: type 21 or Hafnarjeva pot 21 and OpenStreetMap or Slovenia's address register (GURS) finds the building. Because an address is looked up rather than guessed, an offer carries the settlement the register really files it under — so where a street belongs to the town that absorbed your village, picking it corrects the place as well. Each offer reads place · address, so you see both before you take it. This one needs online lookups; an imported gazetteer stops at settlements and knows no house numbers.

Place map

When a person's events (their own and their families') carry coordinates, a small map appears under their events: each geocoded event is a colour-coded dot (hover shows the event and year), and the dots are joined chronologically by the person's life path with direction arrows. A toggle hides or shows it, and a compact colour legend keys the colours. Coordinates come from the file or from Geocode places (§10); the same opt-in base map — or the built-in offline outline — as the Places map applies.

Family relationships

The panel shows three family sections:
  • Parents — + Add Father / + Add Mother search for an existing person or add a new one. The − glyph detaches the role without deleting the record.
  • Partners — + Add Partner creates a spouse family. Each shows the marriage event and adds Engagement, Separation, Divorce, a partnership Status and a custom event via + Add Family Event. The − on the partner's card splits a wrongly-attached spouse from just this marriage — it removes only this spousal bond, leaving both people and all their other relationships (parents, other marriages, children) untouched. Families carry their own notes and links too.
  • Children — listed under each spouse family. + Add Child adds an existing or new person; − unlinks a child without deleting them.

Same-sex couples. A same-sex couple's two parent cards are labelled Parent rather than Father/Mother. To record one, add a partner or parent as usual and set the new person's sex. Behind the scenes GED Merge keeps the standard GEDCOM layout (one partner in each spouse role), so the file stays compatible with other genealogy software.

A person attached to nobody

The four buttons above all attach the new person to the one on screen. When there is nothing to attach them to — the first ancestor of a branch you have not connected yet, or the very first person in an empty file — use the ＋ Add new person button in the top bar, or press N. It works from any mode: Edit opens on the new person with the name field focused, and you link them up later with the same + Add Father / + Add Partner buttons, which also search for an existing person.

Two other places lead here. A search that finds nobody (/) offers to add the name you typed — the given name and surname are split the way your name-display order writes them, so correct it in the name field if the guess is wrong. And a file with no people at all shows the same button in place of the person panel.

Notes, sources & photos

Individuals and families can carry free-text notes, sources and photos. + Add Note and + Add Source reveal their inputs (the Add Source dialog is covered in §13); once a link is present the ↗ glyph opens it in a new tab. Adding and captioning photos is covered in §12.

Detach vs delete

Detach (the − glyph) only removes a family connection — both individual records survive and can be re-linked later. Delete Person (the 🗑 at the foot of the panel) removes the individual and unlinks them everywhere. It asks for confirmation first, but — like every edit — it joins the undo history: CtrlZ brings the person back at their original position, right up until you download.

Warning

Undo reverses a deletion only while you're still working. Once you download and that file becomes your new baseline, the removal is baked in — so keep a backup before a large clean-up.

Saving

Save GEDCOM opens a preview of exactly what changed, then downloads the edited file — see §14. Your loaded file is never modified.

9Merge — compare & combine

The match list

With both files loaded, the Matches list shows every candidate pair with its score and — if a start person is set — its relationship distance. Selecting a row opens the side-by-side comparison. Step through with ← / →.

Filtering

The filter button (top-right of the panel) shows or hides the filter controls, and the header reports how many candidates pass them (e.g. 74 of 114). Narrow the list with:

  • ≥ score — a minimum match-score threshold
  • New persons — only matches that could bring in new ancestors or descendants
  • New data — only matches that add new field data
  • New links — only matches that add or change attached links
  • Differences — only matches with conflicting fields
  • Search name — filter by given name or surname (CtrlF reveals the controls and puts the cursor here)

Columns & sorting

Each row shows the candidate's name and life span, then a set of count columns. Every column header is also a sort button — click it to sort by that column, click again to reverse.

ColumnWhat it shows
ScoreThe match score, 0–100.
DistanceKinship hops from your start person — shown only when a start person is set.
New personsIncoming-only ancestors and descendants this match could bring in. Confirming the merge doesn't import them — choose which to bring in from the Compare Tree.
New dataNew fields the incoming file would add.
New linksAttached links the incoming adds or that differ.
DifferencesFields present in both files but differing.
StatusYour decision — confirmed, rejected or deferred (round status badge; see §15).

Comparing & resolving conflicts

The compare panel highlights only fields that add new data or conflict. For each conflicting field, choose which value to keep:

  • M — keep the Main file's value
  • I — take the Incoming value
  • B — keep Both

Marking each match

Give every pair a decision, by button or shortcut: Confirm (C), Reject (R), or Defer to decide later (D). Once decided, the person carries a round badge of that decision's first letter — C, R or D — on the match row, in Edit and on every chart (§15). Only confirmed matches are merged on save.

Reject means “not the same person” — use it on a false-positive candidate so it stops cluttering the list. Rejected pairs disappear from the match list immediately; only unmarked, confirmed and deferred candidates stay shown. To bring one back, either CtrlZ to undo the rejection, or open that person in Edit (§8) and click Reject again to clear the status — the match reappears in the list either way.

Compare Tree

Compare Tree (V) shows a matched person's ancestors and descendants from both files in one diagram, so you can be sure they're really the same person. Nodes are colour-coded: match, minor differences, major differences, main file only, incoming only. From here you can re-root on any person, mark a status, or use + Bring ancestors / + Bring descendants to graft a whole incoming branch into your main file on save. The Chart settings, zoom controls, overview minimap and Export menu all work here just like the other diagrams (see §11) — except there's no GEDCOM export, since a compare tree can include incoming-only people not yet in your main file.

Merging

Save GEDCOM previews every confirmed match and edit, then downloads the merged file — applying your field-level choices to a copy of the main file and pulling in any new individuals and families connected to those confirmed matches. Full detail in §14.

10Tools — maintaining your main file

The Tools tab (T) runs checks and clean-ups across your entire main file. Each tool shows what it found, or what it would change, before you accept it. Health check's one-click fixes, a Find duplicates merge, a Places rename and Geocode places write into your main file as you work — each undoable with CtrlZ, and saved like any other edit. Normalize, Privacy and Organize sources instead hand you a separate file to download. Either way the .ged on your disk is never rewritten: every save is a new file in your Downloads folder (§14). Six tools sit in the picker at the top — Health check, Find duplicates, Normalize, Privacy, Sources and Places. Two more open from inside the last two: Organize sources from Sources, and Geocode places from Places.

Health check

Scans every individual and family for data problems and groups them by category: no name, no sex, no vital dates, disconnected records, broken or one-way family links, duplicate pointers, pedigree loops (a person who is their own ancestor), sex vs. role conflicts (a female recorded as a husband, or a male as a wife — a genuine same-sex couple, one partner in each role, is not flagged), two spouses in one role (a family that packs two different people into the husband or wife role, hiding one partner — the finding names who is hidden), two sets of parents (a person who is a birth child in two families at once — the finding names both couples, and adoptive or foster links, whether marked with a pedigree type or by an adoption event, are not flagged), future-dated events, death before birth, implausible ages (at death, at marriage, parent's age, spouse age gap), and children by two fathers at once — a mother whose children with one partner are interleaved in time with her children by another, which usually means a child or a whole sibling set hangs off the wrong family (consecutive marriages and adopted children are not flagged). Detached groups catches what a single disconnected person doesn't: up to nine people linked only to each other and to nobody else in the file — usually an import that never got attached, or a duplicate of a branch you already have. One finding per group, on its youngest member, so you can open them and walk the group's relatives; the file's main tree is never a group. A separate GEDCOM structure pass flags encoding, bad lines and levels, stray CONT/CONC, version, unknown or custom tags and unparseable dates — many with a one-click Fix. When a media folder is connected, Check media folder lists referenced media files that are missing from it. It also lists places with coordinates in some records but not others — a place name geocoded on most events but missing its coordinate on a few (common when an event is added or re-typed after the file was geocoded) — and offers to copy the coordinate the value already carries in the file onto the events that lack it, no lookup needed.

Click any issue to open the record in Edit. Batch fixes handle the common cases in one step: Fix all broken links clears dead and one-way family pointers between people and families, Remove dangling references clears every other pointer whose target record is missing — source, note and media citations, associations, and family links nested inside an event — Remove duplicate pointers de-duplicates repeated links, a sex fix sets the sex on unspecified spouses (husband → male, wife → female), and Fill coordinates from the file settles the split-place coordinates above. (For places that have no coordinate anywhere, use Geocode places, which looks them up.)

A broken link can also be repaired one at a time: every broken link finding carries its own Remove link button, which drops just that one dead pointer. Use it when the rest of the list needs a closer look — a link to a family that no longer exists cannot be seen or removed in Edit, because the missing family draws nothing there. Like the batch fixes, it is undoable.

The check runs once per file, so findings you have since repaired in Edit stay on the list until it runs again. Run again, at the top of the panel, re-checks the whole file; when the file has changed since the last check, the button is highlighted and says so.

Find duplicates

Compares every person against every other using the same engine as Merge, and lists likely duplicate pairs with their score. Expand a pair for a side-by-side comparison, then Merge to keep the surviving record and fold the other into it — family links are re-pointed automatically, and the action is undoable. The record you keep is always the one on the left, and the confirmation names both records with their GEDCOM ids so it is clear which one is about to go.

Every field carries the usual keep/take/both buttons, with one exception: Parents is a single choice for the whole block, since a person has one set of parents. It starts on the left record's parents — or on the candidate's, when the left record has none — and you can switch it to the candidate's, or to both for a genuine adoptive or step family.

Where each side's parents (or spouse) are themselves separate records — a whole branch entered twice — the hint under the comparison names them with their own match scores. Tick the ones that are the same person and they are merged first, as part of the same undoable step: their two families then collapse into one on their own, so no link has to be dropped and no one is left disconnected. Merging in that order is what keeps the branch whole; the names also stay clickable if you'd rather open a pair and look at it first. Nothing is ever deleted except the record you confirm — everyone else keeps their record, their facts and their own family.

Children that are a separate record on each side get their own list, just below. They are the opposite case: the merge does bring both of them into the one surviving family, and then leaves them there as two children with the same name and the same birth. Tick the ones that are the same person and they are merged after the pair — by then they share one set of parents, so there is nothing left to choose. Only children that score as a likely match are offered, because two children on one line may genuinely be two people: a name given again to a later child after the first one died is common in old registers, so read the score and open the pair if you are unsure.

On large or heavily-merged files the list starts at a high score (≥ 85) so it stays short and actionable; use the score picker to lower the bar and reveal weaker matches. Related records are grouped into clusters — a run of the same person entered several times (a “parallel branch”) folds into a single header showing how many records and pairs it holds. Unfold it to review the pairs one by one, or Reject all to reject the whole group at once (undoable). Every pair shows both records' GEDCOM ids next to the names, so two people with the same name and dates are still easy to tell apart.

Normalize

Detects the file's own dominant date format, place layout and link style, then re-renders records that deviate — e.g. 1 Jan 1900 → 01.01.1900, reshaping places, and rewriting Matricula or Geneanet links to a consistent form. A preview lists examples of each change before you download the normalized copy.

Privacy

Produces a redacted copy of your file for sharing or publishing, with living people protected — your live main file is never touched. GED Merge infers who is likely living: anyone with no death, burial or cremation event whose estimated birth is recent enough. The estimate comes from their own birth year, or — when that's missing — from dated relatives, or finally a configurable assume-living fallback. A breakdown shows how many were flagged by birth date, by relatives, or assumed.

You control what happens to each flagged person:
  • What to do — sanitize them in place, remove the person, or remove the person and all descendants.
  • Replace each name — with “Living”, custom text, initials, initial + surname, or surname only.
  • Also remove from each flagged record — dates & places (all events), notes, sources, photos & media, or contact details.
  • GEDCOM restriction — strip the data and mark a RESN privacy tag, strip only, or mark only.
  • Also privatize people who died within a chosen number of recent years.

File-level options strip data that isn't tied to one person: the submitter record (your own name, address and email), external tree ids (_UID, _FID, AFN), and every postal address, email or phone number across the whole file. A preview counts how many people are redacted or removed before you download the redacted GEDCOM.

Sources

Browse every repository, source record and media object, grouped by repository. Selecting one shows which individuals and families cite it, each a link into Edit; unattached media and link-only citations are listed separately. A Potential duplicates view groups records that describe the same media, source or repository under different ids — pick groups to collapse and download a deduplicated file with every citation re-pointed.

＋ Add source creates a new source record on its own — cited by nothing yet, for material you want kept in the file before it backs a fact. It opens the same dialog Edit uses to cite a source (§13): paste a link or citation and the fields fill themselves, recognized archive sites included; a link the file already holds is pointed out instead of added twice. A Repository dropdown shows where the record will be filed — the recognized site's repository is preselected (or offered as a ＋ new one), any of the file's existing repositories can be picked instead, and a call number can ride on the link (GEDCOM's CALN, the archive's own shelf mark). The record appears in the tree under its repository with a usage count of 0, and the add is undoable like any edit.

Every source row also carries ✎: edit the record's title, author, place, filing number, repository, link or note in the same dialog — a link edit retargets the source's page image — and a source nothing cites offers Remove there too. Repository rows have their own ✎ covering the archive's name, postal address, phone, e-mail, link and note, and a repository holding no sources yet stays listed with a 0 count. All of it is undoable like any edit.

Organize sources

Organize sources gathers the archive links scattered around your file — Matricula Online, Geneanet, Find a Grave, BillionGraves, FamilySearch, SIstory.si, dLib.si, newspaper and library URLs sitting in link fields, notes or inline citations — and turns them into proper source records with page citations, grouped by book, grave or record and reusing the sources and repositories your file already has. It reads a growing list of sites: from the URL alone it derives a readable title, filing number and place, and Fetch source details can enrich each with the real register/grave/record metadata through public relays (the online-lookups opt-in). Any date a site reports is written in your file's own date format, whatever shape the page used. Citations land on the matching event (created if missing), a QUAY quality can be set globally with per-row overrides, and graves fill the burial place and cemetery address.

Every proposed group is reviewable before you download: edit a source by hand with ✎ on its row (title, author, archive, place, filing number, year range — hand-edited sources are then skipped by Fetch source details), or mark a group Remove references (🗑) to strip a dead or obsolete URL from everywhere it appears without creating a source (↩ unmarks it). The tool honors your Settings › GEDCOM format overrides and re-scans when you change one, and it shares the download with the source duplicate finder — one save applies both. Private people, notes and media are never turned into a citation (§18).

Places

Lists every place in a country → locality → house → address hierarchy, with a usage count per place. Open a place to see the records that use it, or use Rename on any level to bulk-rename or merge a place segment across every record at once — ideal for standardizing spelling variants. A place value that carries its house number (Krupa 7) is filed under the settlement like any other, with the house as a level below it, so a file that numbers houses in the place still browses by settlement.

A place the file has a coordinate for shows it beside the count, so browsing the tree also tells you what is still ungeocoded. It is the coordinate written for that exact level: a place named only by its region carries the region's. Click a coordinate to open a small map of it under the row — the quickest way to catch a place that has quietly been pinned in the wrong country.

Where the file's own records disagree about where a place is, the row shows the prevailing coordinate followed by ⚠ 2 spots, and its map pins every one of them with the number of mentions sitting there — twelve on the church and one 2 km off is a typo, not a second church. Coordinates a few metres apart are one spot, not a disagreement, so a re-geocode never raises the flag. Settling it is Geocode places' job, and the map links straight there.

Geocode places

Geocode places matches your file's place names against an offline gazetteer and writes the coordinates you accept into the GEDCOM as standard MAP LATI/LONG — the same coordinates the Places map and the Edit-view person map then draw from. Every write is reviewed by you and undoable.

Load the gazetteer with one click from OpenStreetMap (country code + Download, needs the online-lookups opt-in in Settings › Advanced); with Download Slovenia (GURS) — all 6035 settlements from Slovenia's national register of spatial units, the authoritative choice for Slovenian research, and it brings the bilingual Italian and Hungarian names with it, so Isola finds Izola. Each settlement carries its občina, which is what tells the two Soteska apart — Soteska (Kamnik) and Soteska (Dolenjske Toplice) both match the name perfectly, and only the municipality separates them; when your place names the municipality too, the right one wins outright. It is listed on its own as SI-GURS, so it sits alongside an OpenStreetMap or GeoNames SI import instead of replacing it — the official settlements and the hamlets only OpenStreetMap knows then both count, and a settlement both describe is offered once, at its official coordinate. Or load a gazetteer by importing a GeoNames country file (.zip or .txt) — the loaded gazetteers are listed under their own heading, and the data stays in your browser. Loading a gazetteer is a one-time step — it lives in your browser, not in the file, so it survives reloads and serves every file you open; once something is loaded the whole loader folds into a single line naming what you have, which you can open again to add or remove a gazetteer. Proposals are ranked: coordinates other occurrences of the same place already carry come first, then exact and historical-name matches (Laibach → Ljubljana), then fuzzy ones. When a place names its country, matches are confined to that country, so a foreign place is never matched to a loaded gazetteer (Belfast, Northern Ireland won't become Bela in Slovenia). Select confident bulk-picks only the unambiguous matches.

All three lists on this page read the same way: a row names its place (and its address, where the row is about one), opens on a click, offers its map only when you ask for it, and collects your picks until the list's own button at its top writes them in one undoable step. The map shows candidate places as clickable pins, your chosen coordinate highlighted, and the file's other known coordinates as faint dots, so same-named places are told apart by position; click anywhere else on the map to hand-pick a point. A row also offers alternatives, a typed coordinate, or a remembered “no match” mark, and a ✎ button to rename the raw place value (all occurrences at once — with an optional place + address split, and the correction is rescanned for matches immediately). That rename box is the same place field the Edit view uses (§8): it completes from the places your file already writes and, for one it has never written, ends with Look this place up in the registers. Picking a register offer fills in the whole place chain — and writes the coordinate that register puts it at — so a misspelt or never-resolved value is corrected and placed in a single step. What the offline gazetteer can't resolve, two per-row online searches cover, both behind the same online opt-in and only on the button click, with their results joining the row's candidates and the circles on its map. Search OpenStreetMap uses OpenStreetMap (Nominatim) — best for street addresses. A value that names a house number in a country Slovenia's address register covers (Šentvid pri Stični 23) is not listed here at all: it is a building rather than a place, so it is reviewed under its settlement in Addresses with a house number below. Search the Genealogical Gazetteer (GOV) uses the Genealogical Gazetteer (GOV), whose entries carry historical, multilingual place names, the place each one is part of (so four same-named Osredek are told apart by their municipality) and a stable GOV id; accepting a GOV match also writes that id into the file as _GOV (a sibling of MAP), a durable link back to the GOV record for other genealogy software. Accepted matches are cached, so other files resolve instantly.

Below the place list, Addresses with a house number covers houses rather than places, and it finds them wherever your file keeps them: in the event's own address beside a settlement place (Kranj + Kidričeva cesta 38), as the whole place value (Črni vrh 35), or packed in among the other parts of one (Kranj (Slovenija), Stražišče 114 - župnija Šmartin). Each is filed under the settlement it belongs to, so a file that writes no address lines at all reviews exactly like one that does. They are grouped by place + address, so one review covers every event at that house, and looked up in the same register. The coordinate is written as ordinary PLAC MAP on those events — nothing non-standard, so every other program reads it. The list is grouped by place and collapsed, busiest first, because a real file holds hundreds of addresses: open a place to see its own addresses, look them all up in one go, and read them off a small map of every house found there — neighbours on one map make an odd result obvious at a glance.

A row is one house, not one spelling: where your file writes the same house several ways — the same number under two different parishes, say — they become a single row, and accepting it writes the coordinate to every one of them. When an old record keeps more than one number because the numbering changed (Hafnarjeva pot 21a / 53), every number is looked up and both houses are offered — the record does not say which is which, so the choice is yours. Each row also carries the same green pin as an Edit-view event: click it to type or paste a coordinate, pick a point off a map, or search OpenStreetMap — the way to place a house the register does not know. Where the place named is the settlement left once the house number is set aside, the group is marked in the place: coordinates go to the value your events actually carry, and Move to another place… is not offered there, because that value is the very text holding the number.

Sometimes a place turns out to hold a hamlet that is really a settlement of its own — a file that records every Klošter 12 under Gradac, Metlika, Slovenia, often because that is the post office. Renaming the place would move the genuine Gradac events too, so each place group offers Move to another place… instead: tick the addresses that belong elsewhere, type or pick the destination place, and only those events are moved. The destination box is the same place field as everywhere else, so the hamlet your file has never spelled out can be looked up in the registers and picked whole, chain and all. Their address lines stay as they are, and the moved events are placed at the destination's own coordinate — the register offer's, or the one your file already records for that place. When neither knows one, they keep the coordinate they had, so a move never leaves an event without a position. Only the _GOV id is dropped, because it names the place they just left. One undoable step, like every other tool edit. The panel opens pre-filled with what the addresses themselves suggest.

For Slovenian places the register settles it. When a house number is not found under the settlement the place names, it is looked up again as a settlement in its own right, and if that is where the register files it the group says so — 6 addresses are in Klošter (Metlika), not Gradac, Metlika, Slovenia — with a button that opens the move already filled in. This uses the same register lookup and the same online opt-in, and only ever runs for places whose country is Slovenia or unstated.

That means one settlement can hold different coordinates on different events, which is correct: two addresses in Kranj are two places. Differing addresses are therefore never reported; the real error is the same place and the same address carrying two different coordinates, and Same place and address, different coordinates lists those at the top of this page. Expand a row for a map of its coordinates, each circle numbered with how many events sit on it — twelve on the church and one two kilometres off is a typo, not a second church. Choose the right circle (or its radio button) and Use this coordinate for all N events rewrites every event of that place and address to it, in one undoable step. When none of them is right — a maternity hospital recorded at three different guesses is none of the three — click anywhere on the map, or type or paste a coordinate, and that becomes the choice. The Health check's coordinate fill likewise copies only within one address, so a house's position can never spread across a settlement.

In Edit, an event with a place shows a green pin after it — solid when the event carries coordinates (hover for the numbers), faded when it does not. (A file that records no coordinates at all shows no pins; coordinates are introduced through the tool above, and the pins follow.) Click it to place that one event, on its own small map: take a coordinate the file already uses for this address or place, look the house up in the register, search online (OpenStreetMap — for an address outside Slovenia, which the register does not cover), type or paste a coordinate, or remove the one it has. This is where to sharpen an address whose settlement is already pinned, since the Tools list only offers places missing a coordinate altogether. When the file holds other events at the same place and address, the panel offers — ticked by default — to write the pick onto all of them, since one house has one position; unticking keeps the coordinate on this event alone. The offer stands even when those events already share a coordinate, which is how a whole address is moved to a better position at once. (The copy is its own undo step, so one undo takes the other events back and a second undo this one.)

Choosing a place or address that the file already knows brings its coordinate along, so a newly typed event is placed in one step — the house's own coordinate when you pick a place + address pair, the settlement's otherwise, and never overwriting a coordinate the event already has. The file-info card reports how far coordinates reach (Coordinates: 16 548 of 17 195 places), which is the quickest way to see whether a geocoding pass is worth making.

Part IV — Shared Features

11Charts, maps & reports

One Charts button (V) — in the Edit toolbar, the person header's chart icon, or a Compare Tree node's panel — opens the full-page Charts hub for the selected person. A tab strip switches between eight kinds (digits 1–8 jump straight to one); GED Merge remembers whichever you used last. Re-rooting a chart — a node's Set as root, a +7 pill, a search hit off the chart — counts as a step, so Back (the button or the browser's) retraces the people you rooted on and only then leaves the hub:

  • Tree, Grid, Fan and Circle — the four pedigree styles. Tree is the tidy layered family tree; Grid aligns generations into columns (a pedigree grid for ancestors, an indented outline for descendants); Fan and Circle are radial ancestor charts (ancestors only — there's no descendants view to fan out).
  • Timeline — the immediate family as lifespan bars along one shared year axis; see below.
  • Relationship (S) — traces how the selected person is related to your start person (§7); if none is set yet, it prompts for one inline. The diagram lays out the chain of relatives between the two, labelled with the exact kinship (e.g. 2nd cousin once removed). When more than one route exists, the Available paths menu switches between the shortest path and a bloodline through a shared ancestor. Click either endpoint's ✎ to swap that person, click any node to re-centre, or open a node in Edit.
  • Map — every event of the branch whose place carries coordinates, clustered on a real map, with optional historical overlays and life paths; see below.
  • Report — a text-based Ahnentafel or descendant register; see below.

The pedigree kinds, the Map and the Report also carry an Ancestors / Descendants toggle (each option shows a live count). The Chart button (the ⚙ gear) opens a settings panel whose options adapt to whichever kind is open; every choice is remembered across sessions:

  • Alignment (Tree, Grid and the Relationship diagram) — lay the chart out Left → Right or Top → Bottom. The radial Fan and Circle have no alignment to choose.
  • Generations — how far the chart reaches from its root person. Step down from All to draw only the nearest few generations; the reading is “4 of 9” — how many are drawn of how many exist. It is one shared choice: the Tree, Grid, Fan, Circle, the Report and the Map all follow it. Where a chart is cut short, the last person drawn carries a +7 pill counting the relatives above or below them that aren't shown — click it to re-root the chart on that person and carry on down (or up) the line. The page header and every export then read 4 of 9 generations, and a shortened Report closes with a line naming the generations it left off.
  • Person — toggle what each node shows: Lifespan, Age, Place, Photo (when a media folder is loaded), Kinship (the relationship label to your start person; not drawn on Fan/Circle) and Badges (the working-state letters — see §15 — on by default). This group isn't shown while the Report or the Map is open; switch to another kind to change it, and the Report picks up whatever it's set to.
  • Marriage — add the marriage Date and/or Place to each couple's connector (the fan / circle collar, or beside the Timeline's ⚭ markers). Both off by default.
  • Events (Timeline only) — whose bars carry event dots: This person, All people or Off; plus Event labels (compact text under each dot) and the shared Residence bar (see Reports, below).
  • Facts and Structure (Report only) — see below.
  • Privacy — Hide living people redacts anyone inferred to be living, showing only their relationship or “Living” (see §18), so the diagram is safe to share.

The Map's own filters — event kinds, the year window and life paths — sit in a filter bar on the map page itself rather than in this panel; see Map below.

Find in chart answers “where in this diagram is that person?”. Type a name in the search box at the right of the controls row (CtrlF puts the cursor in it) and the canvas moves to that person and flashes them the moment your typing settles — no key to press. A shared ancestor is drawn once per line of descent, so the counter says which of their places you're on, and ‹ › — or Enter and the ⌕ button — step through the rest. If nobody by that name is drawn here but somebody in your file matches, the box offers that person — click their name to redraw the chart around them. Every kind has the box, the Compare Tree (§9) included. On the Map it matches place and street names as well as people, then flies to the event and opens it; on the Report it scrolls to the person's numbered entry — and there CtrlF is left to your browser's own find, which searches the whole text. It's the counterpart to the file-wide search (/), which always re-roots the diagram on whoever you open.

Repeated people. The same person can reach the root down more than one line — cousins who married each other, or a couple whose both partners descend from the root person. Their branch is drawn at the first position only; every later position still shows the person and their marriage, but is left empty and carries a → badge — in the box's bottom-right corner on the Tree and the Grid, at the outer edge of the wedge on the Fan and the Circle, where the ancestors would have continued. Click it to go to the position that does carry the branch (see §15). Nobody is drawn — or counted — twice, so the Ancestors / Descendants counts are counts of people. The Report follows the same rule: a person met a second time keeps their first number and is shown as a cross-reference to it.

Use the zoom controls to zoom in and out, reset to 100%, or fit the whole diagram to the screen; large diagrams also show an overview minimap — drag the canvas to pan (on a touch screen, pinch with two fingers to zoom). Every kind carries an Export menu, always led by GEDCOM — it downloads just the people currently drawn, as a standalone file a friend can merge into their own tree. What follows depends on the kind: the diagrams offer SVG (a sharp, infinitely scalable image) and PDF (asks which paper you're printing on, then opens your browser's print dialog, where you choose Save as PDF); the Map offers PNG instead; and the Report offers a plain Text file, an RTF document that opens in Word or LibreOffice, and PDF.

Printing a wide diagram

A family diagram that pans comfortably on screen usually can't be printed on one page: fitting it to A4 shrinks the names past reading. So PDF always asks for the paper first — A4, A3, A2, A1, A0, Letter or Custom — and fits the chart to it; asking the printer for a page the size of the diagram only gets you whatever the driver falls back to, with most of the chart missing. On the Tree and Grid kinds the same dialog adds a print size, because those two can be cut into several sheets, and it tells you how many sheets the diagram will take; Print then sends the whole set to the print dialog, one sheet per page. The big formats are for a plotter or a copy shop, and a wide chart that wants a dozen A4 sheets often prints on one A1. Custom takes any size in millimetres, so a plotter roll — say 1100 × 2000 mm — prints as a single enormous sheet; a custom size is used exactly as you type it (width × height), so the orientation switch doesn't apply to it. Whatever the paper, the set is scaled to fill it, and the dialog says what that comes to — Prints as 3 sheets · at 62% size. Print size is the setting that matters most: an ancestors chart doubles in width with every generation, so Large (readable at arm's length) can want several times the sheets of Small (a wall chart in fine print), and One sheet refuses to cut at all — the whole diagram is shrunk onto the single sheet you chose, however small the names end up (watch the percentage). Change it and watch the sheet count — and remember that Generations in the chart settings, and turning off the place, photo or kinship rows, shrink the diagram before it is ever split. The diagram is cut at its branches, never mid-box: a branch that doesn't fit carries on over a sheet of its own, and the person it was cut from keeps a numbered green circle — 3 — showing which sheet picks the line up. Every sheet's header names it (Sheet 3 of 7 · continues Ana Novak from sheet 1), and all sheets print at the same scale, so boxes are the same size throughout and the printed set tapes together into one chart. A long, narrow lineage is cut across a generation instead, so deep lines don't waste a sheet each.

Timeline

The Timeline kind draws the selected person's immediate family as horizontal lifespan bars on one shared year axis: parents (and a step-parent row, if one remarried) above, the person's own generation — siblings and half-siblings interleaved by birth order — beside them, then each union's spouse and children (and step-children) below. Small glyphs mark dated events on each bar (* born, ~ baptized, † died, ▭ buried, ⌂ residence, ⚒ occupation, ✎ education, →/← emigration/immigration), and ⚭ marks a marriage. An open-ended bar (no death recorded) tapers off — to the last dated event, or to the present day if the person is presumed living.

Map

The Map kind plots every event of the chosen branch whose place carries coordinates (the standard GEDCOM MAP · LATI/LONG structure under PLAC — written by the Geocode places tool, or already present in your file). Markers are coloured by event kind (birth, marriage, death, burial, residence, other) and nearby events cluster into counted dots: click a cluster to zoom in, or — once it's small enough — to open its event panel, listing each event with year, place and person links (with kinship chips).

  • Filters — a bar above the map, not part of the chart settings: event-kind chips, a two-thumb year window slider (with exact year inputs), and an Undated toggle for events without a date.
  • Paths — the chip beside the event kinds draws each shown person's life path: their chronological stops joined by a line with direction arrows. Click a path (or a person's 〰 button in the event panel) to single it out — the rest dim, the legs get numbered, and a name chip appears over the map.
  • Base map — the background every map draws on, chosen in Settings › Map › Base map. Five need no account: the default plain CARTO style, which follows the light or dark theme; plain OpenStreetMap, which names the most hamlets, farms, chapels and cemeteries; a terrain map whose contours and hillshade show the valley a family stayed in and the ridge that separated two parishes; and Esri's satellite and topographic views — the first answers whether the house still stands, the second prints well. Custom URL… takes your own {z}/{x}/{y} template instead, whose licence and credit are then yours to honour. The credit shown on the map and burned into PNG exports follows the choice. A sample map above the picker draws the current choice — together with any layer marked Default — so you can compare the styles before leaving Settings. Ticking Default on a layer takes the sample to the ground that layer covers and to a zoom it is drawn at, so neither a French map nor a layer that only appears house by house is switched on into an empty frame. Drag and zoom it freely; Fit brings the sample back.
  • Overlays — extra map layers drawn over the base map. Configure them once in Settings › Map › Additional map layers: a name, a tile URL template with {z}/{x}/{y}, the years the map depicts, and its required attribution (subscription sources such as Arcanum/Mapire work with the tile URL from your account). Tick WMS for an OGC WMS service instead — then the URL is the service endpoint (…/wms), you name the layer(s) to draw, and an optional extra-params box carries things like a time-enabled layer's TIME= year. The Add a free preset… menu includes Slovenia's national GURS maps (historical and current orthophoto, topographic map, cadastral parcels, cadastral municipalities, house numbers, settlements, municipalities) and historical surveys: the Habsburg Spezialkarte 1:75 000 (1877–1918) complete — all 805 sheets of the monarchy, from Bohemia and Galicia to Dalmatia and Transylvania — France's Carte de l'État-major, Switzerland's Dufour and Siegfried maps, and — reaching furthest back — Schraembl's Generalkarte von Deutschland of 1797, the German lands, the Alps and the northern Adriatic in the Holy Roman Empire's last years. At 1:530 000 the 1797 map is period context rather than a survey: it is placed by its own engraved graticule, so towns can sit a few kilometres from their modern coordinates — the map's own error, faithfully kept. Some layers only draw at certain zooms — the cadastre and house numbers when you zoom in close, settlement and municipality names and outlines when zoomed out; the historical orthophoto shows one survey year (change it in the layer's extra params if a spot is blank). The topographic map is the colour Osnovna karta the national land-survey viewer itself draws: one layer that stays legible from the whole country down to 1:2500, where individual buildings, contours, field boundaries, paths and toponyms appear — usually the layer that settles which house an ancestor lived in. A second preset adds the older black-and-white Temeljni topografski načrt 1:5000, which records the buildings and field patterns of its own, earlier survey; it draws only when you are zoomed in close. Several GURS layers answer a click on the map: house numbers give the full official address (street, number, settlement, post code), a parcel gives its number, cadastral municipality and area, and the cadastral-municipality layer names the katastrska občina and its number — the unit land and parish records are filed under. The map's Overlays chip then lists your layers with an opacity slider each, and highlights the ones whose period matches the selected year window — the map of your ancestor's own time. The order of the list — in Settings and in the chip, with the ▲ ▼ buttons to change it — is the stacking order on the map: the first layer draws on top. Keep a thin reference layer (house numbers, parcels) above a full-page historical map, or it disappears under it. Tick a layer's Default box in Settings to have it switched on wherever a map is drawn: every Places map opens with it on (untick it in the Overlays list for that map only), and the small per-person and geocoding maps show it too — clicking those small maps answers with the layer's details as well, so a house number can be read without leaving the person.
  • Fit — the third button under the map's zoom controls frames everything currently plotted, on the Places map and on the small per-person, geocoding and Settings-sample maps alike; after following an overlay across the country it brings the whole set back in one click. Hovering a marker names the place it stands for (a single event also shows its street address).
  • Privacy — the base map is opt-in: until you allow tiles (one click on the map, or Settings › Map), the map draws on a built-in offline world outline and nothing leaves your device. The chart-settings Hide living people toggle removes their markers and paths entirely.
  • Export — PNG snapshots the current view (tiles, overlays, paths and markers, with the required attribution burned in); GEDCOM exports the drawn people as a standalone file.

A small per-person map with the same colours (and the person's life path) also appears under the events list in Edit — hide it with its own button above the map (§8).

Reports

The Report kind renders a text-based, numbered report instead of a diagram — the same Ancestors / Descendants toggle as the pedigree charts switches between two classic genealogical formats:

  • Ahnentafel (Ancestors) — the standard ancestor numbering: the root person is no. 1, their father 2n, their mother 2n+1, grouped by generation. A generation's heading shows its entry-number range and how many of its ancestors are actually known (e.g. 3 of 4 known). A person who appears twice through a shared ancestor (pedigree collapse) shows once in full, with later appearances as a clickable “→ same as no. X” link back to the original.
  • Descendant register (NGSQ) (Descendants) — sequential numbering in the order each person appears; every generation groups children under a clickable “Children of NAME and SPOUSE” heading that jumps back to the parents' entry.

A second toggle switches the writing style:

  • List — compact numbered fact lines (the Timeline's glyph vocabulary).
  • Narrative — the same facts as flowing prose paragraphs, book-style, written in your interface language. Sentences use the correct tense for people presumed still living, and Slovenian narrative correctly declines dates and pronouns (place names are never inflected). Sources become numbered footnote markers on the sentence they support; short notes are woven in parenthetically, longer ones become their own footnote.

The Facts group in Chart settings adds optional lines (or narrative sentences) per person: Age, Occupation, Education, Residence (shared with the Timeline's Residence bar), Notes and Sources (with a link to the cited page or image, where one resolves). Structure adds a Table of contents. Click any entry to open its detail panel, re-root the report on that person, or jump to Edit. Export GEDCOM (everyone currently listed), a plain Text file, an RTF document for Word or LibreOffice, or PDF — all of them carry the same headings, numbering and facts as the page.

12Photos & media

GEDCOM files reference media files by name rather than embedding them, so to display them GED Merge needs the folder they live in. Connect a media folder from the file-info panel (or accept the prompt offered when a file references local media). Photos (JPEG, PNG, SVG, …) show as thumbnails; PDF documents (scanned records) show as a 📄 tile and open in the built-in PDF preview. The folder is read locally — your files never leave your device.

Viewing

Click any thumbnail to open the full-screen lightbox. When a person has several photos, step through them with ‹ / ›, the arrow keys, or the thumbnail tray; Esc closes. For a shared media object the info panel also lists the records that reference it, each a link into Edit. When a photo records a marked region for this person (a crop within a group photo), GED Merge highlights that area so you can tell who is who — and the person's thumbnails (tray, cards, chart nodes) show just that region, so a group photo works as their portrait.

Adding & editing

In Edit, Add media attaches an image from your media folder. In Chrome and Edge you can also drag a file in, or use Import from disk… in the picker — a file from outside the folder is copied into it first (the browser asks once per session to allow writing). In the lightbox, the info panel is editable: change a photo's title, date (the date the image depicts), place and description — each field saves on blur and joins the normal undo/redo history. ⛶ Mark person's region (under the fields) lets you drag a rectangle on a group photo to mark this person — it becomes their thumbnail and the highlighted region above. The × control removes a photo from the person; the image file on disk is kept.

13Sources & citations

+ Add Source — on a person, a family, or an individual event — opens the Add Source dialog. Paste a full citation or just a URL: GED Merge fetches the page title where it can, and detects when your citation matches an existing source in the file, adding the new page to it rather than creating a duplicate. You can fill in structured fields — title, author, periodical/series, publisher, agency, filing number, page, URL and note — as needed for your house style.

Add Source recognizes archive links the same way Organize sources does: paste a URL from a supported site (Matricula, Geneanet, Find a Grave, FamilySearch, newspaper and library archives, and more) and it prefills the title, author, place, archive, filing number and page, with the online lookup fetching the real book/grave/record details. The created source picks up your file's place format, year range and repository. Which layout it writes — where citations, page images and baptism sources sit — follows your Settings › GEDCOM overrides (§2).

Existing citations can be reopened and edited the same way. To see and tidy sources across the entire file — including duplicates — use Tools › Sources, and to convert scattered links into proper citations in bulk, Organize sources (§10).

14Saving & the change report

Looking for the Ahnentafel or descendant-register report? That's the Charts hub's Report kind (§11) — this section covers saving your main file and its change log.

Saving is one unified flow for both edits and merges. Save GEDCOM (CtrlS) opens a preview listing every change — persons and families changed, fields applied, new records added, and anything that could not be merged. Each entry carries a badge: New, Incoming, Edited or Removed. Use Remove from Save to drop any single change before downloading. The preview covers every event, whatever its tag — events outside the common list (adult christening, LDS baptism, marriage banns, vendor-specific events) show their changes too — and written place coordinates appear as the event's changed field.

Download GEDCOM writes two files: the new .ged and a plain-text change report documenting exactly what was applied. The downloaded file becomes your new working baseline inside the app, so what you see next matches what you just saved.

The download is always written as UTF-8, and its header is set to say so. A file that arrived in an older encoding (ANSEL, ANSI, Windows-1250, UTF-16) is therefore converted, and the preview — like Health check — says which encoding it came from. The conversion is what keeps accented letters correct, but it does change a line other software reads, so after your first save of such a file, check that it still imports into the programs and services you use. Characters that could not be read from the original at all show as “�”; saving cannot bring those back, so correct them in Edit first.

Tip

Your original file on disk is never overwritten — the download is always a new copy. Even so, keep a backup of your tree before large merges — see Backing up & versioning below.

Backing up & versioning your tree

Because every save is a fresh download, it lands in your Downloads folder — which quickly fills with near-identical files and no clear “which one is current?”. A little discipline turns that into a safe, recoverable history of your tree.

The one habit that matters. Keep one dedicated folder for your tree (for example Family tree). After each save, move the downloaded .ged out of Downloads into that folder, and next time load that file back as your main file — never work from scattered copies in Downloads. To make this painless, GED Merge stamps every saved file with the date: Smith-tree.2026-07-22.gedmerge.ged. Successive versions therefore sit side by side in date order and never overwrite one another; the newest is your working copy, the older stamps are your history. Loading a stamped file back in is fine — the next save simply carries today's date.

Below are the two approaches as complete, repeatable cycles — load → change → save → store — so you can see exactly what happens every session. Pick one; both share the golden rule above.

The cloud-folder cycle (recommended for most people)

Put your family-tree folder inside a cloud-sync service you already use — Dropbox, OneDrive, Google Drive or iCloud Drive. With no new software to learn, you get an off-computer backup (your tree survives a lost or broken laptop) and automatic version history (these services can restore an earlier version of any file).

Set up once:

  1. In your sync app, make one folder for your tree — for example Family tree.
  2. Put your current .ged in it. Don't have one yet? Save once from GED Merge and move that file in — it becomes your starting point.

Every editing session:

  1. Load. Open GED Merge. If it reopened where you left off, you're ready; otherwise drag your latest .ged from the Family tree folder onto the drop zone (§3).
  2. Change. Edit records, merge an incoming file, or run a tool as usual.
  3. Save. Press CtrlS → Save GEDCOM, check the preview, then Download GEDCOM. Two files land in Downloads: the dated .ged and its change report.
  4. Store. Move the dated .ged from Downloads into your Family tree folder. (Keep or discard the report — it's just a human-readable log, not needed to reopen the file.)
  5. Done. Your sync service backs it up and keeps its own history. The date in the filename keeps every version in order; next session, load the newest one.

To undo a bad session: re-load an earlier date-stamped .ged as your main file, or use your sync app's version history / restore previous version on the file.

The Git cycle (advanced)

GEDCOM is plain text — one fact per line — so it fits Git, the version-control tool developers use, unusually well: you see exactly which people and events changed between two versions and roll back with confidence. If you're comfortable installing a tool, this gives the strongest history.

Set up once:

  1. Install GitHub Desktop (Windows or macOS) and sign in with a free GitHub account.
  2. File → New repository. Name it (e.g. family-tree) and pick a local folder — this becomes your one tree folder.
  3. Put your .ged in that folder and rename it to a stable name such as tree.ged.
  4. Type a summary (“Initial tree”) and click Commit. When you Publish the repository, choose Keep this code private — your tree holds details about living relatives, so never publish it to a public repository (§18), or redact it first with the Privacy tool.

Every editing session:

  1. Load. Open GED Merge and drag tree.ged from the repository folder onto the drop zone.
  2. Change. Edit, merge or run a tool as usual.
  3. Save. Press CtrlS → Save GEDCOM → Download GEDCOM. The dated .ged lands in Downloads.
  4. Store. Move it into the repository folder and rename it back to tree.ged, replacing the old one. GED Merge stamps the download with the date, but Git records the date and message of every version itself — so a fixed filename gives a clean, line-by-line diff instead of a brand-new file each time.
  5. Commit. In GitHub Desktop the changed lines appear (which people and events changed); type a short summary (“Merged Novak branch”, “Fixed birth dates”), click Commit, then Push to back it up on GitHub.

To undo: GitHub Desktop's History lists every version to compare; right-click a good commit to revert back to it.

Which should I use?

If you just want your tree backed up and recoverable, the cloud-folder cycle is all you need. Reach for Git only if you want to inspect exactly what changed between versions, or to collaborate. Either way the golden rule is the same: one dedicated folder, and always load your latest saved file back as the main file.

18Privacy

All reading, matching, merging and editing happens locally in your browser, on your own computer — including your media folder, which is read on your device only. Your family tree is never sent to a server. The full Privacy Policy & Terms are also linked in the footer below.

What can leave your device

Two settings, both off by default, are the only features that make a network request at all. Neither ever sends your file: what goes out is the one value being looked up, or the map area being drawn — never a person, a date or a record.

  • Online lookups (Settings › Advanced) — sends the text you are looking up. A source's URL, to read back its page title: this one goes through a public relay, because the archive sites block a browser from reading their pages directly, and Settings names the relays it will try. A place name or house number, sent straight to OpenStreetMap, Slovenia's address register (GURS) or the Genealogical Gazetteer (GOV) when you press one of the search buttons in Geocode places or pick Look this place up in the registers while editing. And a country code, when you download a gazetteer in one click. Nothing is sent until you ask: the buttons are the trigger, not the typing.
  • Base-map tiles (Settings › Map) — every map, from the Places map down to the small one under a person's events, draws on square tiles fetched from the provider you choose, which tells that provider roughly where you are looking. Leave it off and the maps draw on a built-in offline outline of the world, with no request at all. Your coordinates stay in your file either way — the map fetches the ground, not your markers.

Accepted lookups are remembered in this browser, so the same place is not asked about twice; Clear locally stored data forgets them again (§2). Everything else — parsing, matching, merging, the charts, the reports and every tool — runs with no network at all, which is why the app keeps working offline once loaded (§2).

The Privacy Policy lists each of those services by name, says what is sent and what is stored where, and the Terms cover what you take on when you enable them.

Marking records private

GED Merge understands the privacy flags other programs write — MacFamilyTree's PRIV, MyHeritage's _PRIV Y, and the standard RESN privacy / confidential. People, families, notes and media items show a 🔒 toggle in Edit that sets or clears the flag (written in your file's own dialect; a compare file's flags are converted to that dialect on load, like dates and places). Private entries are honored throughout: a private note's URL never surfaces as a link chip, and Organize sources and Add Source never turn a private person's, note's or media item's URL into a citation. In the merge comparison a Private row shows the flag, and it is carried into the merged record.

Sharing without living people

To share a tree or a chart, two features hide people who should stay private. Charts and reports have a Hide living people option (§11) that redacts anyone inferred to be living — and anyone explicitly marked private — showing only their relationship or “Living”. For a whole file, Tools › Privacy (§10) produces a redacted copy: it infers who is likely living, flags declared-private people too, and lets you sanitize, rename or remove them and strip file-level identifiers before download.
